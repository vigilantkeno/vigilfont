Typeface vs font: the distinction that stopped mattering in 1985

It was a real difference once, and it described a real object. Here is what it meant, and why almost nobody needs to keep score.

Aa

A typeface is the design. A font is the thing you set it with. Garamond is a typeface. Ten-point Garamond Italic, as a drawer full of metal, was a font. That distinction was precise, useful and physical for about four hundred years, and then digital type quietly dissolved the reason for it.

Where the words came from

“Font” comes from the French fonte — something melted, something cast. That is exactly what it was. A punchcutter cut each character at a specific size, a matrix was struck from it, and molten metal was cast into that matrix. The output was a physical set of sorts: every letter, numeral and mark you needed, at one size, in one style.

So a font was inventory. If a print shop owned Caslon in 10-point roman, 10-point italic and 12-point roman, it owned three fonts of one typeface. Wanting the same design a little larger meant buying more metal, because there was no way to scale a piece of lead.

The typeface, meanwhile, was the design itself — the decisions a punchcutter made about how an a should be shaped. It existed across every size, and it belonged to whoever drew it.

A typeface is what you see. A font is what you use.

What broke it

Phototypesetting came first, in the 1950s and 60s, projecting characters from film through a lens. One filmstrip could now be enlarged or reduced, so a single “font” covered a range of sizes for the first time. The link between a font and one specific size was already loosening.

Then PostScript arrived in 1984, the Apple LaserWriter shipped in 1985, and type became mathematics. An outline described by curves scales to any size at all. The physical object the word “font” had named for four centuries — the case of metal at one size — simply stopped existing.

What replaced it was a file. And because the file is the thing you buy, install, license and email to a printer, “font” attached itself to the file, and then, inevitably, to the design inside it.

What the words mean now

Typeface
The design. The intellectual work — the shapes, proportions and rules that make Futura recognisably Futura at any size.
Font
The file that delivers the design. In practice also the common word for the design itself, which is not an error so much as a change.
Font family
All the related styles that share one design: the weights, their italics, and any extra widths or variants.
Weight
How heavy the strokes are — Light, Regular, Bold, Black. Usually numbered 100 to 900.
Style
Roman or italic. A true italic is drawn separately, not slanted by software.
Variable font
One file containing a continuous range rather than fixed steps. This is where the old distinction quietly returns.
Glyph
One drawn shape in the file. Distinct from a character: “fi” is one glyph and two characters.

A worked example

Vigil is one typeface. Its family contains eight weights from ExtraLight to Black, each with a matching italic — sixteen styles — plus a separate Outline style in roman and italic.

Shipped as individual files, that is twenty fonts. Shipped as variable fonts, it is two: one roman file and one italic file, each carrying the entire 200–900 weight axis as a continuous range. Same typeface, same family, same eighteen styles. Two files or twenty, depending on how you want to load it.

That sentence is only sayable because the words still mean different things. It is also the clearest case for keeping both of them around.

When it still matters

  • Licensing. You license fonts, not typefaces. A licence usually grants a specific number of installations of specific files, which is why the file is the unit that counts. In most jurisdictions the letterform designs themselves are far harder to protect than the software that draws them.
  • Production. “Send me the font” means send the file. “Which typeface is this?” means name the design. Asking the wrong one gets you the wrong answer.
  • Variable fonts. Once a single file spans a hundred weights, “font” means something genuinely distinct from any one of the designs inside it.

The verdict

The distinction is real, and it is worth understanding if you work with type, because it explains why licences are shaped the way they are and why a family can be two files or twenty.

It is not worth correcting people over. The word “font” absorbed the broader meaning because the object it originally named stopped existing, and language does that. Anyone who says “I love this font” is being understood perfectly. Where it matters — a licence, a handoff, an invoice — say which one you mean and move on.
