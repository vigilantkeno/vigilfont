The Stranger Things font is ITC Benguiat

Drawn in 1977, twenty years before the show is set to remember it. The brief was twelve Stephen King paperbacks in a padded envelope.

1977

It's ITC Benguiat — a decorative serif drawn by Ed Benguiat and released by the International Typeface Corporation in 1977. The title treatment stretches its serifs and tightens the fit, so what you see on screen is a customised setting rather than the typeface straight from the box. But the bones, and all of the feeling, are Benguiat's.

The brief was a stack of paperbacks

When the Duffer Brothers briefed the title sequence, they didn't describe a typeface. They sent twelve vintage Stephen King book covers and asked for that style and that feeling.

That is a better brief than most typographers ever get, because it specifies the association rather than the artefact. It doesn't say "serif, condensed, glowing red." It says: make me feel the thing I felt looking at a rack of paperbacks in 1983.

The studio that answered it was Imaginary Forces, with creative director Michelle Dougherty. What they found when they went looking through those covers was that a single face kept recurring — because ITC Benguiat had been all over King's 1980s editions. The show didn't borrow a mood and then find a font. It followed the mood back to the font that had been generating it all along.

Why a 1977 typeface says "1983" so well

ITC Benguiat is loosely based on Art Nouveau letterforms, though it is explicitly not an academic revival — Benguiat took the flavour of the period, the swelling curves and the high waistline, and drew something that had never actually existed. It is a 1970s idea of an older thing.

That double distance is exactly why it works. The show is a 2016 idea of the 1980s, whose paperbacks were a 1980s idea of the 1970s, whose type was a 1970s idea of 1900. Nostalgia stacked four deep, and it reads as authentic because each layer was sincere when it was made.

There is a lesson in that for anyone reaching for a retro face. The reason Benguiat evokes the era isn't that it looks old. It's that it looks like a specific era's taste in looking old.

The sequence is doing something harder than it looks

The title itself is close to still. Letters drift in from the edges, red on black, and lock into place while the synthesiser climbs. There is no camera move to hide behind, no imagery, nothing happening but type assembling itself.

Dougherty has described the work as concentrating on the type and finding the most beautiful combinations of it — which sounds modest and is not. A sequence with nothing in it but letterforms has nowhere to put a weak decision. Every frame is the typeface, so every frame is either working or exposed.

What the drift buys is unease. The letters begin disjointed, slightly wrong, not yet a word, and the resolution into STRANGER THINGS arrives as relief a half-second before the music confirms it. That is a typographic effect, not a cinematic one. The tension comes from withholding legibility and then granting it.

It is also the reason the logo survives outside the sequence. Because the treatment is nearly the typeface itself — extended serifs, tight fit, no illustration — it still reads on a phone icon, a T-shirt, a birthday cake. Title designs that lean on effects usually don't travel. This one is just letters, and letters travel.

You have been reading it your whole life

Once you can see ITC Benguiat you cannot stop. It is on the 1980s Stephen King covers, obviously. It is also on the Choose Your Own Adventure books, which means an entire generation absorbed it before they could name a typeface at all. It is on The Smiths' Strangeways, Here We Come. It runs through Quentin Tarantino's title sequences, including the cast names in Pulp Fiction. It has appeared in Star Trek film titles.

That is a strange career for a piece of lettering: children's gamebooks, horror paperbacks, an English guitar band, and a director who quotes the seventies on purpose. The common thread isn't genre. It's that all of them wanted to feel like a slightly disreputable memory.

The man who drew it

Ed Benguiat, 1927 to 2020, drew more than six hundred typefaces. Tiffany, Bookman, Souvenir, Edwardian Script, and the Benguiat family that carries his name. He was central to the founding of the International Typeface Corporation, the first independent licensing company for type designers, and worked with Herb Lubalin on U&lc, the magazine that taught a generation of designers what type could do.

He died on 15 October 2020, aged 92, which means he lived to watch a typeface he had drawn forty-three years earlier become the visual signature of one of the most-watched shows on television. Not many designers get that. Most typefaces that end up defining a decade do it long after the person who drew them has stopped being able to enjoy it.

Six hundred typefaces, and the one everybody knows is the one that spells a town in Indiana that doesn’t exist.

What the letters are actually doing

Strip the red glow and look at the drawing. ITC Benguiat is high-contrast for a display serif — thick stems against hairline-ish thins — with a waistline pushed unusually high, so the crossbar of the E and the joint of the R sit above the optical centre. That is the Art Nouveau inheritance, and it is what gives the face its slightly stretched, top-heavy poise.

The serifs are the signature. They are not the crisp wedges of a Times or the slabs of a Rockwell; they flare, curving out of the stem rather than meeting it at an angle. In the show's treatment they are extended further still, which is the one real intervention — pull those flares out and the letters start to interlock, which is why the logo reads as a single object rather than fourteen characters.

It is also, honestly, a difficult face. That high waist and heavy contrast make it uncomfortable below about thirty pixels, and the flared serifs clog at small sizes. ITC Benguiat is a poster face wearing a book face's serifs, and it should be treated as one. The people who set body copy in it because they liked the show are finding this out.

Can you download it?

Not for free, not legitimately. ITC Benguiat is a commercial typeface and is licensed through its publisher. The results promising a free Stranger Things font are, with few exceptions, either unlicensed copies of Benguiat's work or loose imitations drawn to resemble it — and the imitations tend to fall apart exactly where the original is doing its best work, at the serif joins and the terminals.

If you need the real thing for a real project, buy the licence. It is a hundred-year-old idea drawn by a master and it is still earning its keep; paying for it is not a hardship.

If you want the feeling without the licence, look for openly licensed faces with genuine Art Nouveau bones rather than a Benguiat knock-off. You will get something honest instead of something that will look wrong at large sizes and get your project a takedown notice.

What a type designer takes from this

The Stranger Things logo is often held up as great type design. It isn't, quite — it's great type selection, which is a different and in some ways harder skill.

Nobody drew new letterforms to make you feel 1983. Someone recognised that the feeling already lived inside a forty-year-old typeface, and had the discipline to use it nearly straight, stretch the serifs, and get out of the way. The work was in the looking.

That is the part worth stealing. A typeface arrives carrying everywhere it has already been — every paperback, every album sleeve, every gamebook a nine-year-old read under a duvet. You cannot draw that in. You can only find it, and then decide whether the baggage is the thing you want.

Which is also the argument for knowing what category a face belongs to before you pick it. Vigil is a geometric sans and carries geometric baggage: Bauhaus, modernity, confidence, a certain coolness. It would be a terrible choice for a horror title, and no amount of good drawing would fix that.
