The Spotify font is Spotify Mix

Eighteen months of work by a Berlin foundry, and a counter shaped like a transmitter. It replaced Circular — it is not a version of it, whatever the font blogs tell you.

pbd

Spotify uses Spotify Mix, a bespoke typeface launched in May 2024. It was drawn by the Berlin foundry Dinamo with Spotify's in-house creative team over roughly eighteen months, and it replaced Spotify Circular — the customised Circular the company had run on for years.

That last clause is where almost every other answer to this question goes wrong.

The error worth correcting first

Search this question and you will repeatedly be told that Spotify Mix is "a custom variant of Circular with modified letterforms and spacing." It is not. Mix replaced Circular. It is a different typeface, by a different foundry, drawn from scratch.

The mistake is easy to reconstruct. Spotify genuinely did use a customised Circular, genuinely does now use something called Spotify Mix, and a site summarising both without checking dates will happily glue them into one sentence. Then the next site copies it. Within a year the invented version outnumbers the real one.

This is the failure mode of the entire "what font is that" genre. Nobody checks. Everybody copies. A plausible sentence hardens into a fact.

It is the same mechanism that made a 2003 film-prop font into America's supposed licence plate typeface, and the reason no honest source will tell you the exact weight of the Nike "Just Do It" lettering.

What came before: Circular, and the font of the 2010s

Circular was designed by Laurenz Brunner and published by the Swiss foundry Lineto. Spotify adopted a customised version of it during the mid-2010s and ran on it for the better part of a decade.

Circular is worth understanding because of how thoroughly it defined a period. A geometric sans with a large lowercase, tight even spacing and a friendly, slightly rounded feel, it became the house look of an entire cohort of technology companies — most famously Airbnb. For a stretch of years, "modern tech brand" and "Circular or something close to it" were nearly the same statement.

It earned that. Circular solved a real problem: it kept the clean geometry that reads as modern while enlarging the lowercase enough to survive a phone screen, which is exactly the compromise the older geometric faces refuse to make. It was warm without being cute, and it held up at interface sizes where stricter geometry falls apart.

Which is precisely the problem a company eventually runs into. A typeface you license is a typeface anyone can license.

What Dinamo actually built

Spotify Mix is not a subtle refresh. The stated idea is mixing: it deliberately blends conventions from geometric, grotesque and humanist sans-serifs rather than committing to one genre, which is meant to echo the range of music and podcasts on the service.

The details that matter to a type designer:

  • A transmitter shape in the counters. The enclosed negative space in letters like p, b and d is not a plain oval — it is shaped to suggest audio radiating outward. A brand metaphor buried inside the whitespace of a letter, where most people will never consciously notice it.
  • A wide variable range, condensed through extended. Spotify's brand design lead framed this as emotional range: moving from condensed to extended lets the same words carry a different tone.
  • Flicks and diagonal cuts on stroke terminals — the humanist inheritance, and what keeps it from reading as another neutral geometric sans.
  • Alternates and multiple numeral sets, including plainer forms for moments when expressiveness is unwanted.
  • Drawn for motion. It was built expecting to be animated, which is a design constraint that barely existed for typefaces twenty years ago.

But the logo is a separate question

Plenty of people searching for the Spotify font do not want the interface typeface at all. They want the wordmark — the Spotify in the green.

That is its own object, and the general rule holds: a logotype is a fixed drawing, not a font. Once a wordmark is approved it stops being type and becomes artwork, with its spacing, proportions and any quirks frozen into a vector file. Setting the word in whatever typeface the brand uses elsewhere will not reproduce it, because the logo has been adjusted in ways no font can apply automatically.

So "what font is the Spotify logo" has the same answer as most versions of that question: none, exactly. It is lettering, drawn once and kept. The typeface question and the logo question look identical from a search box and are two entirely different things — which is a large part of why answers to both are so consistently muddled.

Why a company builds its own typeface

Commissioning custom type is no longer exotic, and the reasoning is consistent.

Licensing at scale gets expensive and awkward. A retail licence covers a defined number of users, installs, page views. Multiply that across apps, web, television, out-of-home and dozens of markets, and the accounting becomes a standing obligation.

A licensed face carries no ownership. Circular was excellent and it was everywhere. There is no equity in looking like your competitors.

Off-the-shelf families rarely cover the range. A product surface needs a condensed weight for a cramped label, an extended one for a hero image, tabular numerals for durations, and the whole thing interpolating smoothly for animation. Commissioning that is often cheaper than assembling it from three families that disagree about proportion.

The contrast with Coca-Cola is instructive. Coke's identity is a piece of handwriting from 1886 that has barely moved. Spotify's is a variable font system with alternates, rebuilt from scratch after a decade. One brand's typography is a relic it protects; the other's is infrastructure it replaces.

What a type designer takes from this

The interesting decision is not the drawing. It is the range.

Mix is built to be a toolbox rather than a voice: wide enough in width and weight that the same family can whisper or shout, with alternates for when the personality needs turning down. That is a genuinely different brief from the one most typefaces are drawn to. A text face wants to be consistent and invisible. A brand system increasingly wants to be a controllable instrument.

Variable font technology is what made that practical. Shipping condensed-through-extended as one interpolating file, rather than forty static cuts nobody installs correctly, is the difference between a system designers use and a system they route around.

The second thing worth noticing is that Mix's most distinctive feature is in the counters — the holes. Counters are where a typeface's character actually lives, far more than the outer silhouette, and they are the first thing to close up at small sizes or low contrast. Putting the brand idea there is a confident bet that the family will mostly be seen large.

What you can actually use

Spotify Mix is proprietary. It is not sold, and it will not be. Circular can be licensed from Lineto, and it remains a genuinely good typeface. Anything offered as a free Spotify font download is an unlicensed copy of Circular, which is piracy with extra steps.

If what appeals is the category rather than the brand, plenty of geometric sans-serifs are free and legitimate. The field guide covers the options, and Vigil is one of them — a geometric sans with open counters and a large lowercase, free under the SIL Open Font License.

What Vigil will not do is imitate Spotify Mix, and it is worth saying plainly. Mix is a wide variable system with genre-mixing terminals and alternate sets, built by a commercial foundry over eighteen months for one company's specific surfaces. Vigil is a straight geometric sans for people who need good type and have no budget. Different problems. Reaching for a free font to imitate a bespoke one is the reliable way to end up with something that looks like a near-miss, and a near-miss reads worse than an honest difference.
