Fonts similar to Avenir — and what they miss

Avenir is already an answer to a question. Substitute it badly and you undo the thing it was built to fix.

oat

Montserrat is the closest free alternative to Avenir, with Nunito Sans the better pick for interfaces and long text, and Mulish the most restrained of the three. All are free under the SIL Open Font License. But the useful part of this question is not the list — it is knowing which of Avenir's properties you cannot afford to lose.

Because almost every "fonts similar to Avenir" list makes the same mistake: it recycles the Futura list. And Avenir is not a Futura-style geometric sans, which is the entire point of it.

Avenir was built to break the geometry

Adrian Frutiger designed Avenir in 1987; Linotype released it in 1988. The name is French for future, and the reference is not subtle — it is a direct nod to Futura and the circle-based sans-serifs of the 1920s.

But Frutiger did not draw another one of those. He took the geometric idea and then deliberately violated it in three specific places:

  • The vertical strokes are slightly thicker than the horizontals. A true geometric sans is monolinear — every stroke the same weight. That looks clean and reads poorly, because the eye uses weight differences to tell letters apart.
  • The o is not a perfect circle. It is very slightly narrowed. A perfect circle is wider than every other letter in the alphabet and pulls the rhythm apart.
  • The ascenders are shortened. Futura's long extenders are beautiful and waste vertical space; Avenir's restraint is what lets it set tightly without collisions.

None of those are compromises in the pejorative sense. They are the design. Avenir is Futura's clarity with the readability problems engineered out, which is why it survives at 14px and Futura does not.

o a t Where Avenir shows its hand: a narrowed o, a double-storey a, a t with a real hook. Shown in Vigil, which resolves all three the same way for the same reasons.

So the wrong substitutes are the strict ones

This is why the advice to replace Avenir with Jost, or with any rigorously geometric free face, gets it backwards. Those are good typefaces and they are solving the opposite problem — they restore the 1920s purity that Frutiger spent a career arguing against.

Swapping Avenir for a strict geometric sans re-introduces every legibility issue Avenir exists to fix. It will look similar in a logo and worse in a paragraph.

If what you liked about Avenir was the circles, you actually liked Futura, and you should go and use Futura. If what you liked was that it stayed calm and legible through a long block of text, you liked the corrections, and you need a substitute that makes the same ones.

Why the question gets asked at all

Almost nobody goes looking for an Avenir substitute because they dislike Avenir. They go looking because of how type is sold.

Avenir is commercial, and commercial type is not licensed the way software usually is. A desktop licence covers a fixed number of workstations, so a team that grows has to re-buy. A webfont licence is typically metered on monthly page views, so a site that succeeds gets a larger invoice. An app licence is separate again, and so is anything embedded in a PDF sent outside the company.

For a studio billing a client, that is a line item. For a one-person project, a nonprofit, or anyone shipping something that might quietly get popular, it is an open-ended liability attached to a design decision made in week one. That is the actual reason these searches exist, and it is a completely rational reason.

It is also worth saying plainly: the foundries are not being greedy. Type takes years to draw and there is no other way to get paid for it. But an honest answer to "what looks like Avenir" has to acknowledge that most people asking have a budget of zero and need a face they will never have to re-license.

The free ones worth using

Montserrat, by Julieta Ulanovsky, released in 2011 and later redrawn by Jacques Le Bailly, is the usual best answer. It was drawn from the painted signage of the Montserrat neighbourhood in Buenos Aires rather than from any Swiss model, which gives it a slightly warmer, sturdier personality than Avenir. It is a touch friendlier and a touch less neutral. In display sizes the swap is close to invisible.

Nunito Sans is the flat-terminal companion to Nunito, which Vernon Adams drew with rounded terminals for display use; Jacques Le Bailly extended the family and produced the non-rounded version. Nunito Sans is the one to reach for in interfaces and long text — open apertures, a generous but controlled lowercase, and subtle stroke modulation that does the same job Frutiger's does.

Mulish, also begun by Vernon Adams and released under the OFL, is the most minimal of the three. Its one conspicuous divergence is a single-storey a, which is exactly the feature Avenir does not have. If your text is short, that reads as a stylistic choice; across a page it changes the texture noticeably.

All three are on Google Fonts, so hosting and licensing are non-issues.

Set side by side, the ranking depends entirely on the job. In a headline, Montserrat wins — it has the presence and the closest overall colour. In a paragraph at 15px, Nunito Sans wins, because its aperture and spacing were tuned for screens and Montserrat's wider forms start to feel loose. Mulish is the safest choice when the type should disappear entirely, provided that single-storey a does not bother you.

One thing none of them reproduces: Avenir's particular evenness across the weight range. Frutiger's family was planned as a system from the start, so Light, Book, Medium and Heavy sit at genuinely even intervals. The free families grew more organically, and their middle weights tend to cluster. If your design depends on a clear step between two adjacent weights, check that specific pair before committing.

The one nobody mentions: you may already own Avenir

Worth checking before hunting for a replacement at all. Avenir Next ships with macOS and iOS. Frutiger reworked the family with Linotype's Akira Kobayashi from 2004, expanding the weights and widths and improving the fitting for digital use, and that reworked family is bundled with the operating system.

That covers desktop and print work on a Mac outright. It does not cover webfont use — a bundled system font is not a web licence, and self-hosting it would be a licensing violation. But a great many people looking for an Avenir substitute for a document, a deck or a poster have it installed already.

How to check a candidate in five minutes

  • Set 100 words at the real size. Not the alphabet. Avenir's virtues are text virtues and do not show up in a headline.
  • Look for stroke modulation. Zoom into the o. If the sides and the top are identical in weight, it is a strict geometric and will not behave like Avenir in text.
  • Check the a. Avenir's is double-storey. A single-storey a is an immediate, unmistakable difference.
  • Measure the line width. Same sentence, both faces. Montserrat runs wider than Avenir; if your layout is tight, that is the constraint that will bite first.
  • Test the numerals. Especially if anything tabulates — prices, times, data.

What a type designer takes from this

Avenir is the clearest case in modern type of a family whose defining features are its deviations. Describe it by its system and you get "geometric sans-serif", which is nearly useless. Describe it by where it departs from that system and you get the actual typeface.

That generalises. The thing that makes any family feel like itself is usually not the rule it follows but the handful of places it declines to. A substitute chosen on the rule will always disappoint, because the rule is the part every other typeface in the genre shares.

There is a second, more practical lesson, which is that readable and pure pull against each other and somebody has to lose. Frutiger made that trade explicitly, in public, with his name on it. A lot of type decisions are the same trade made accidentally.

Where Vigil sits

Vigil makes the same corrections Avenir does, for the same reasons: a double-storey a, a lowercase large enough to survive small sizes, counters kept open rather than geometrically ideal. If you are looking for an Avenir substitute because you need geometric character that holds up in body text, that is the shelf Vigil is on.

It is not an Avenir clone and will not pass as one. Vigil is softer at the corners and its geometry is more overt; Frutiger's proportions are the product of decades of signage work and are more finely tuned than mine. On a project where Avenir is already approved and licensed, keep Avenir.
