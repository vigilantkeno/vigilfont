License plate fonts: shaped by a die, aimed at a forger

Every other typeface is drawn for a reader. This one is drawn for a stamping die, a camera, and someone with a roll of black tape.

FE

There is no such thing as the license plate font. Road signs in the United States run on a single federal standard. Plates don't: every issuing jurisdiction picks its own, so North America alone carries dozens of variants, and a plate from Ontario and a plate from California are not speaking the same alphabet.

What they share isn't a design. It's a set of adversaries. A plate typeface has to survive a stamping die, resist a criminal with tape, and satisfy a camera — and only incidentally be read by a person. No other letterforms in daily life are designed under those conditions.

The die drew the letters

Start with manufacturing, because manufacturing came first. A traditional plate is not printed. It is embossed: a die presses the characters up out of a flat sheet of aluminium, and the raised faces are then inked or foiled.

A die has opinions. It wants shapes it can cut accurately and press repeatedly without tearing the metal or wearing the tool. In practice that meant letters built only from straight lines and circular arcs — geometry you can lay out with a ruler and compass, and later specify exactly in CAD. It also meant a single uniform stroke weight, since a stroke that thins and thickens is a stroke that deforms unevenly under pressure.

That is the entire explanation for why plate letters look the way they look. The flat, industrial, slightly stiff quality isn't a style decision anybody made. It is what you get when the tool is a press rather than a pen.

The typographer Leeward Pro's survey of North American plate fonts — still the most thorough catalogue of these letterforms anywhere — sorts them into four construction types, and once you know them you cannot stop seeing them in traffic:

  • Semicircular — straight sides closed off by half-circles top and bottom. Close cousins of Germany's DIN 1451, usually narrower.
  • Squarish — bowls become boxes with rounded corners, typically true 90-degree arcs. Eurostile's industrial relatives.
  • Oval — elliptical bowls and arcs, the closest these ever get to a humanist sans, though the mechanical construction and even stroke weight keep them firmly in the family.
  • Hybrid — inconsistent, often with one logic for the letters and another for the numerals. Squarish alphas, oval digits.

Germany drew letters against a criminal

The most interesting plate typeface in the world exists because the old one could be edited with tape.

West Germany used DIN 1451, an excellent engineering sans with a serious flaw in this specific job: its letters are too closely related to one another. A strip of black tape or a dab of paint turns a P into an R. An F or an L becomes an E. Against a backdrop of 1970s terrorism and ordinary plate theft, a registration you could edit by hand at the roadside was a genuine security problem.

The answer was FE-Schrift — fälschungserschwerende Schrift, "forgery-impeding typeface" — designed by Karlgeorg Hoefer between 1978 and 1980, introduced in 1994 and mandatory on German plates from November 2000.

F E   P R   3 8 The pairs a forger works on. FE-Schrift's job is to keep each one from becoming the other. Shown in Vigil, not FE-Schrift.

Its method inverts everything a type designer is normally taught. A good text family is built on shared parts — the same bowl in the b, d, p and q, the same shoulder in the n and m — because consistency is what makes a page feel like one voice. FE-Schrift refuses that. The letters are deliberately disproportionate, and no character is derived from any other. Each one is its own irregular object, precisely so that no amount of added ink turns it into its neighbour.

It is a typeface designed to be internally inconsistent on purpose, which is close to heresy, and it works. Several other countries adopted it, and the Mercosur bloc — Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay — standardised on it too.

Britain legislated the stroke width

The British approach was to leave the drawing alone and regulate everything around it.

The typeface is Charles Wright, originally from 1935. The version in force is Charles Wright 2001, adopted by the DVLA as the single legal standard and modified for automatic number plate recognition cameras. Since 2001 it has been mandatory: no substitutions, no clever spacing, with only a 3D-effect variant permitted.

And the regulations are specific in a way typographers rarely see. Characters must be 79mm tall and 50mm wide — the 1 and the I excepted, for obvious reasons — with a stroke width of 14mm.

A stroke width written into law. Most typefaces are lucky to get an opinion; this one got a statute.

The point of fixing the dimensions and the spacing is the same as the point of FE-Schrift's irregularity. If the gaps are legally defined, you cannot shuffle characters to make BD51 SMR read as a name, and a camera always knows where to look.

Then the reader stopped being human

The quiet turn in this story is that the primary audience changed.

For most of the twentieth century a plate was read by a police officer, at distance, in bad light. Now it is read overwhelmingly by machines: ANPR and ALPR cameras at toll gantries, car parks and junctions, running at speed on infrared. Charles Wright 2001 was explicitly adjusted for those cameras. FE-Schrift's separation of similar characters serves them just as well as it serves against tape, because the failure mode of an algorithm and the failure mode of a forger turn out to be the same one: this character resembles that character too closely.

It is a rare case of security and legibility pulling in the same direction. Usually they don't.

The die is gone. The shapes stayed.

Here is the part I find genuinely funny.

Embossing is on the way out. Flat plates, digitally printed on reflective sheeting, are cheaper, lighter and let a jurisdiction put whatever it likes on the background. Every manufacturing constraint that produced those stiff arcs and uniform strokes has evaporated. A flat plate could be set in anything.

And what do the states printing flat plates use? Digital replicas of the embossed fonts. The exact letterforms that a stamping die demanded, now reproduced by a printer that has never met a stamping die.

The constraint became the identity. Those shapes stopped meaning "this is what metal-forming permits" and started meaning "this is a licence plate" — and once a form carries meaning, the engineering reason for it is no longer load-bearing. This is skeuomorphism, but in aluminium, and nobody planned it.

What a type designer takes from this

Two things, and the first is the same lesson the road-sign fight teaches from the other end.

Legibility is not a property a typeface has. It is a relationship between letters and a specific reader under specific conditions — and here the "reader" is a die, a camera, an officer and a criminal at once, wanting contradictory things. FE-Schrift is superb at its job and would make an atrocious book face. That is not a criticism of it. It is the whole idea.

The second is about distinctiveness, and it is more useful than it first appears. A text face gets its evenness from shared parts, and I build that way too — the same curve logic runs through Vigil's b, d, p and q. But the plate designers found the limit of that principle. When it matters most that this letter is not that letter, similarity becomes the enemy, and you have to give each character something no other character has.

Which is why the useful question when you are choosing type for anything that must not be misread — a dosage, a serial number, a code someone will type back — is not "is this legible?" It is "how far apart are the letters that could be confused?" Check the 1, the l and the I. Check the 0 and the O. Check the 8 against the B. A face that keeps those apart is doing the work a licence plate does, and a face that doesn't will fail in exactly the place it costs you.

Can you download a license plate font?

Not the official ones, mostly, and it's worth knowing what you're actually getting. FE-Schrift and Charles Wright 2001 are regulated standards attached to legal specifications, not consumer downloads. Plate manufacture is a licensed business in most jurisdictions for good reason.

What circulate freely are replicas — independently drawn digital fonts based on observed plates, of varying fidelity:

  • Penitentiary Gothic — often mistaken for a historical typeface, it's a 2003 commercial font by Andrew Leman and Richard Lucas, drawn from California plates specifically to make props for film and television. Its extra styles stack in different colours to fake the embossed relief.
  • Driver Gothic — based on Ontario's letterforms.
  • License Plate — Dave Hansen's, drawn from Washington state.
  • Keystone State and Pennsylvania — Pennsylvania-derived.

Use them for what they are: period props, film work, and the pleasure of the reference. Do not use one to make a plate.

Vigil is not a plate font and would be a bad one — it is a geometric sans built for paragraphs, and it shares parts across letters on purpose, which is precisely the thing FE-Schrift refuses to do. What it does take from this corner of the world is the discipline underneath it: open counters, a large lowercase, and a 1, l and I you can tell apart. The counters are where legibility lives, whoever is doing the reading.
