The airport font is Frutiger — and Heathrow ran a serif

One typeface was drawn for an airport and ended up in hundreds of them. The airport that refused it spent a decade proving the received wisdom wrong.

ces

The airport font is Frutiger. Adrian Frutiger drew it for the new Paris airport at Roissy — Charles de Gaulle — and it was released commercially in 1976 by Stempel with Linotype, under its designer's name. It is not required anywhere. No standard names it. Airports simply keep choosing it, which is a better endorsement.

That is the short answer. The longer one: terminal signage is among the least regulated public typography there is, the runner-up is Helvetica, and the world's busiest international airport spent a decade running its signs in a serif, with test data behind it.

A typeface commissioned by an airport

The brief came with a building attached. Paris was building an airport from nothing at Roissy and needed a directional sign system to open with. Terminal 1 — Paul Andreu's circular ten-storey drum ringed by satellites — was inaugurated on 8 March 1974 by prime minister Pierre Messmer. The first commercial flight landed five days later.

The obvious move was to set the signs in Univers. Frutiger had designed it, it was everywhere, and its systematic range of widths and weights was exactly what a sign programme wants. He turned it down.

His reasoning was about the reader's situation, not the letters. Univers was drawn for a page held still at arm's length. An airport reader is walking, dragging a bag, looking up at an angle, in a hurry, often not reading in their first language. The failure mode there is not that a letter looks wrong. It is that letters stop being distinguishable — the round ones collapse toward a common blob, and the word loses its silhouette.

So the new alphabet opened up. The apertures of c, e and s widened so the openings stay open at speed. The x-height rose relative to the caps, putting more of the word at the larger size. Similar characters were pulled apart. It was named Roissy.

c e s a — 1 l I The letters that decide whether a sign works: the open ones, and the ones that turn into each other. Shown in Vigil, not in Frutiger.

The commercial family followed. Stempel, with Linotype, issued it in 1976 as Frutiger.

The date nobody agrees on

Worth flagging, because this gets copied wrong forever: sources do not agree on when the commission arrived. Some say 1968. Some say 1970. Some say 1970–71, with the signage alphabet finished around 1972. That is a two- or three-year spread on the founding fact of the most-used signage typeface in the world, and the pages stating it most confidently are the ones with no source at all.

Not in dispute: the alphabet was drawn for Roissy, it was in place for the airport, and the retail family arrived in 1976 under a new name. Anyone giving you the exact month is guessing.

Heathrow ran a serif, on purpose

Here is the part every listicle leaves out. Through most of the period when Frutiger was becoming the default, the British Airports Authority used a custom typeface called BAA Sign Bembo, designed for Heathrow around 1994 by Shelley Winter with Freda Sack. It is exactly what the name says: a serif, cut very bold, on a high x-height, and never commercially released. Frutiger replaced it only in the Heathrow rebuild of roughly 2006–09.

A serif, in the world's busiest international terminal, for a decade, against everything designers say about signage.

And when Heathrow chose a replacement, somebody actually tested it. Robert Waller's paper Comparing typefaces for airport signs, in Information Design Journal in 2007, combined legibility testing, consumer research and expert review to inform that decision.

The result was not the one the experts predicted. The serif performed as well as the sans in legibility testing. What mattered more than serif or sans was character width — and the condensed sans performed relatively poorly.

The variable everyone argues about turned out not to be the variable. The one nobody mentions — how wide the letters are — turned out to be it.

It is the most useful finding in airport typography and almost never cited, because it is inconvenient. "Use a sans serif" is a rule people enjoy repeating. "Use something wide enough" is less satisfying and more correct.

What is actually standardised — and what isn't

Aviation regulates almost everything, and largely not the letters on a terminal sign. Two things beside it are standardised, and the contrast is instructive:

  • The pictograms. In 1974 the US Department of Transportation commissioned the American Institute of Graphic Arts, which worked with Roger Cook and Don Shanosky of Cook and Shanosky Associates. They surveyed the symbols already in use worldwide, rated them for legibility, international recognisability and resistance to vandalism, and drew 34 symbols, published that November as Symbol Signs. Sixteen more followed in 1979. They are copyright-free. The luggage, the escalator, the figures on the toilet doors: a 1974 federal design project.
  • The airfield. Outside the terminal, signs are specified in detail. The FAA's advisory circulars AC 150/5340-18 and AC 150/5345-44 cover the siting, design and construction of runway and taxiway signs, down to five defined sign sizes with legend heights of 12, 15 and 18 inches for ordinary taxiway guidance.

So the suitcase symbol is standardised, and the letter a pilot reads at 30 knots is standardised, and the typeface a passenger reads on the way to gate B24 is whatever the operator liked. Less an oversight than an admission: the airfield sign has one reader doing one task. The terminal sign has forty million readers doing everything.

The other answer is Helvetica

American terminals lean the other way. The Port Authority of New York & New Jersey commissioned Paul Mijksenaar in 1999 to build one signage system across JFK, Newark and LaGuardia, and what makes it work is not the typeface — it is the colour coding. Yellow for flight information: gates, ticketing, check-in. Green for ground transportation. Black for amenities. The later wayfinding manual, a 2021 SEGD Honor Award winner, describes the lettering as a restyled Helvetica.

Colour carries the category; type carries the name. A passenger scanning for a gate is filtering to the yellow signs and then reading. Get the filter right and the typeface has an easier job than designers assume.

One statistic to distrust while you are here: 75% of the world's airports use one of three typefaces — Helvetica, Frutiger or Clearview. It is quoted everywhere in the same words, and traces back to design trade-press pieces from 2014 and no further. No survey, no methodology, no sample. It may be roughly right. It is also exactly the shape of a number that gets repeated until it is true.

Airports are leaving the shared alphabet

The recent movement is away from the off-the-shelf choice and toward commissioned type, for reasons that have nothing to do with legibility. Amsterdam Schiphol is the clearest case. Bureau Mijksenaar has worked on its wayfinding since 1990, and Frutiger was its signage face for decades. In 2025 the airport replaced it: a new identity by the studio thonik, with a custom sans serif built by the type foundry Bold Monday and called Royal Schiphol Group Variable. The stated objection was not that Frutiger failed. It was that it felt cold — clear and functional, and no warmer than that.

An honest reason, and a melancholy one. A typeface drawn to be invisible under stress was eventually found guilty of being invisible.

What a type designer takes from this

Width is doing more than weight. The Heathrow research found character width mattered more than the serif question, and condensed sans serifs performed worst. Designers reach for a condensed cut whenever space is tight. But the scarce thing on a sign is not horizontal room, it is the reader's certainty about which letter they are looking at, and condensing spends exactly that. The same conclusion turns up in the road-sign research, where the narrowest FHWA series was eventually abandoned as illegible.

Rules about categories are usually rules about specimens. "Sans serif for signage" survives because nobody tests it. When Heathrow did, the serif held its own. That does not make serifs good for signage — it means the category was never the operative variable, and a rule stated at that level will be wrong at random.

The reader's body is part of the brief. Frutiger's design argument was about someone walking, carrying, glancing up, reading a second language. Not one of those is a typographic fact. All of them changed the letterforms. The equivalent question for interface type is not what size the text is; it is whether the person is sitting still.

And the Schiphol decision is where this stops being about legibility at all. Once a typeface clears the functional bar the argument moves to tone, and tone wins eventually. Not a failure of engineering — what happens when engineering succeeds and there is nothing left to fix.

Can you download the airport font?

Not free, no. Frutiger is licensed through Linotype and now Monotype, and the files on free-font sites are unauthorised copies of a typeface somebody is still selling. BAA Sign Bembo was never released at all.

If you want the behaviour rather than the drawing, the useful category is the humanist sans serif: open apertures, generous width, a tall lowercase, characters kept apart. Under open licences that includes Fira Sans, Source Sans 3 and Public Sans. None is a Frutiger clone and none should be sold as one; what they share is the logic, which does the work.

Vigil is not a wayfinding typeface and a terminal is the wrong place for it. It is a geometric sans built for screens and paragraphs, and a geometric skeleton is precisely what the Roissy brief rejected — circles read as circles at distance, the failure Frutiger was drawn to avoid. What carries over is the part that generalises: a large lowercase, counters kept open rather than closed for style, and a 1, l and I you can tell apart. The counters are where legibility lives, at the gate and on the page alike.
