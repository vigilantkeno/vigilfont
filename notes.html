2026-09-04 — Keno Vigil

A vigil is what you keep when you refuse to go to sleep on something.

K.

For more than ten years there was a calendar block on my phone that said MAKE FONT. All caps. Recurring. Set to “free,” which is calendar-speak for this will not happen today either. I started three times. Maybe four. A sketchbook of lowercase a’s here, a half-drawn alphabet there, a folder called font_final_v2 that I’m not going to open in front of you. Every start died the same way: I got the letters I loved and then met the four hundred glyphs I didn’t, and I went back to my actual job.

My actual job, for fifteen years, has been building software for people who don’t have time for bad software. Nurses studying at 11pm. Doctors trying to learn a procedure between patients. Clinical trials that live or die on a form. That work teaches you one thing above everything else: nobody is going to admire your interface. They’re going to read it, fast, tired, on a phone, and either understand or not. Typography isn’t decoration in that world. It’s the whole product wearing a disguise.

So I always knew what I wanted. Not a display face, not a logo with pretensions. A text face. Something you could set a paragraph in at sixteen pixels and forget about, and then blow up to 200 pixels and go, oh. Oh, that’s got a personality.

Where the taste comes from

I grew up in Parsippany, New Jersey, which is to say I grew up reading highway signs, and then in Morehead City, North Carolina, which is to say I grew up reading the sides of boats. Both taught me the same lesson: letters that have to work outdoors, at speed, for strangers, are the honest ones. Geometric bones. Big x-height. No tricks.

Then came the fonts I actually fell for. Gotham’s confidence. Avenir’s quiet intelligence, the way it looks mathematical and isn’t. Montserrat, which taught a whole generation of us what a free font could be. And the rounded ones, which I loved and didn’t trust, because most rounded fonts are just regular fonts that got a little too friendly at the party.

The thing I wanted didn’t exist: a geometric grotesk with the softness applied like an engineer would apply it. On purpose. In specific places. With a rule.

Rounded by rule, not by default

That became the whole design. Vigil rounds most at the exposed terminals, the ends of the C and S and the arms of the E, where a stroke stops and the eye lands. It rounds less at the structural corners. It doesn’t round the punctuation at all, because a period should still be a period.

Soften where you meet people. Stay square where the structure needs you. Don’t blur the periods.

I have come to believe this is also a decent way to live.

Why it finally shipped

Two things changed.

The first is that in June I climbed the height of Everest on a ski hill in Utah over 36 hours, one lap at a time, because I’d signed up for it and the date came. I had also, at some point before that, run a marathon for the same reason. The lesson was not inspiring. It was procedural. Things get finished when the finish line is on the calendar as a fact instead of a wish.

The second is that I stopped pretending a font has to be born from nothing. Every typeface is a fork of the alphabet. Vigil is built on Figtree, Erik Kennedy’s open-source geometric sans, under a license that exists precisely so people like me can take a great skeleton and give it a different body. I didn’t draw every curve from scratch. I decided what every curve should be, which turns out to be the part I’d been avoiding for ten years. The rest is code, patience, and a lot of squinting at the letter K.

It shipped on September 4, 2026. Eight weights, italics, an outline for when you want to shout, and a variable axis that goes from whisper to wall. It is free. It will always be free. No email, no account, no “just tell us where you work.” Download the zip.

What Vigil means

It’s my name, sure. But the word came first. A vigil is staying awake through the long stretch when nothing is happening yet, because you’ve decided something will.

Ten years is a long vigil. Turns out you can keep one and still finish.

Now go set something in it.

— Keno Vigil, Cary, North Carolina

A century of circles: the geometric grotesk

OGa

Three German typefaces appeared within about a year of each other and defined a category that is still with us. Jakob Erbar's Erbar came first, in 1926. Paul Renner's Futura and Rudolf Koch's Kabel followed in 1927. All three drew letters from the compass and the ruler — circles for O, C and G, triangles for A and V, straight strokes everywhere else — in the spirit of the Bauhaus conviction that form should come from construction rather than from the pen.

Futura won. Its perfectly round O and pointed apexes became shorthand for modernity, and it travelled further than any of its contemporaries — eventually to the Moon, on the plaque Apollo 11 left behind in 1969. But the pure geometric sans has a well-known flaw: circles and straight lines don't set comfortably as text. The letters fight each other for space, and long passages feel mechanical.

The second generation fixed that by cheating, gracefully. Herb Lubalin and Tom Carnase's ITC Avant Garde Gothic in 1970 kept the geometry but pushed it toward display, with famously tight spacing and ligatures. Adrian Frutiger's Avenir in 1988 went the other way: he described it as a geometric sans drawn by a human hand, with an O that isn't quite a circle and strokes that thin where they join. Avenir is the ancestor of nearly every "humanist geometric" that followed.

The current wave — Gotham, Circular, Figtree and their many relatives — inherits Frutiger's compromise and adds large x-heights tuned for screens. Vigil sits inside this tradition and makes one more adjustment: it treats the corners as a variable, not a constant.

Soft corners: a short history of the rounded sans

Kzl

Rounded terminals are older than type. Any letter painted with a brush or cut with a router ends in a curve, and signwriters have relied on that softness for as long as there have been shopfronts. In metal and phototype, though, rounding was a deliberate choice, and for most of the twentieth century it was a rare one.

The modern rounded sans is largely a product of the late 1970s. Helvetica Rounded appeared in 1978. A year later, Volkswagen commissioned a corporate face, and VAG Rounded — geometric bones, fully rounded ends — became one of the most recognisable brand typefaces of its era, later travelling well beyond the car company that named it. Arial Rounded followed the same recipe, and for two decades "rounded" meant exactly this: take a sans, round every terminal to the full stroke width, stop.

Screens changed the audience. Rounded sans faces read as friendly and approachable, which is why they became the default for consumer apps and children's products alike. Gotham Rounded, Nunito, Quicksand and Varela Round all arrived in the 2000s and 2010s, and the open-source ones spread everywhere Google Fonts did. Their shared weakness is the one VAG Rounded had: uniform rounding. Every corner is soft, so the I looks like a pill, the 1 loses its flag, and at text sizes the whole page goes slightly out of focus.

Vigil's answer is to ration the softness. Round where a stroke ends in open space and the eye reads it as a gesture. Round less where two strokes meet and the corner is doing structural work. Leave the letters that depend on a hard edge alone. The result reads as a clean sans at 16px and only becomes visibly rounded at display sizes — the reverse of what most rounded fonts do, and the point of the whole exercise.
